Research Note

단계별 통제 개입을 활용한 LLM Agent 오류 복구 병목 식별

국문 초록

LLM 기반 에이전트의 자율적 업무 수행 능력이 확대되면서, 장기 과업에서 발생한 오류를 복구하는 능력이 중요해지고 있다. 그러나 기존 연구는 주로 최종 성공 여부나 궤적에서 관찰되는 오류를 분석해 왔으며, 복구 과정의 어느 단계가 실제 복구를 제한하는지는 충분히 규명되지 않았다. 본 연구는 오류 복구를 탐지, 위치 식별, 진단, 수정 계획, 실행 및 검증 단계로 구분하고, 단계별 관찰 오류 빈도와 동일 실패 상태에서의 단계별 통제 개입 효과를 비교한다. 이를 통해 자주 관찰되는 오류와 실제 복구 병목의 일치 여부를 검증하고, 신뢰성 높은 AI 에이전트 설계를 위한 개선 우선순위를 제시하고자 한다.

Introduction

Large language model (LLM)-based agents are increasingly designed to perform complex tasks through extended sequences of reasoning, planning, tool use, execution, and verification. Unlike conventional language models that generate a single response, agents continuously interact with external environments and make intermediate decisions that influence subsequent actions. This expanded autonomy creates new reliability challenges because an error at one point in a trajectory can persist, propagate, or be transformed into a different error in later steps. Accordingly, an agent’s capability should not be assessed solely by whether it eventually completes a task, but also by how it responds after an error has occurred. Error recovery is particularly important in long-horizon tasks, where successful completion often depends on recognizing a failure, identifying its location and cause, forming an appropriate repair plan, executing the repair, and determining whether the problem has actually been resolved. However, final task success alone provides limited insight into how these intermediate recovery states evolve. A more detailed understanding therefore requires treating recovery as a sequential process and examining whether correctness achieved at one stage is preserved as the agent proceeds to subsequent stages.

Recent research has begun to move beyond terminal success metrics by examining the internal dynamics of agent failures and recovery. Self-correction and verification approaches attempt to improve agent performance by allowing models to critique prior outputs, incorporate execution feedback, and iteratively revise their actions. More recent trajectory-level studies focus on identifying where failures originate and how they affect later behavior. For example, TRAJDEBUG traces the lifecycle of errors in long agent trajectories and distinguishes local errors from those that remain responsible for terminal failure, highlighting the importance of analyzing downstream consequences rather than merely counting observed mistakes. Complementing this perspective, ORACLE-SWE isolates several information signals in software-engineering agents, including reproduction tests, regression tests, edit locations, execution contexts, and API usage, and measures how providing idealized information changes final task performance. Similarly, PROBE structures failed-run telemetry into diagnosis and recovery guidance, but its results reveal a diagnosis-recovery gap: accurate diagnosis does not necessarily lead to successful recovery unless the diagnosis is converted into actionable and executable guidance. These findings collectively suggest that obtaining correct information or reaching a correct intermediate state does not guarantee that subsequent stages will remain correct. In other words, recovery may fail not only because a stage is initially wrong, but also because correctness achieved earlier is lost during later transitions.

Despite this progress, existing studies provide limited explanation of how correctness is preserved or degraded across the successive stages of recovery. Prior work typically identifies critical errors, evaluates the value of particular information signals, or measures whether an intervention ultimately improves task success. What remains less understood is what happens after a recovery stage has been made correct: whether the agent maintains that correct state in subsequent reasoning and action, and at which transition it first regresses to an incorrect state. This distinction is important because two agents may receive the same correct diagnosis yet diverge later. One may translate it into a valid repair plan, whereas the other may form an incorrect plan or execute the correct plan improperly. The present study therefore conceptualizes LLM-agent error recovery as a sequence of stage-level state transitions, including error detection, localization, diagnosis, repair planning, execution, and verification. For failed trajectories, a controlled intervention is introduced at a selected stage to establish a known-correct recovery state, after which the agent is allowed to continue the remaining recovery process. The analysis then examines whether correctness is preserved in downstream stages and identifies transitions in which a correct state changes into an incorrect one. Accordingly, the study asks: (RQ1) How reliably does an LLM agent maintain correctness across successive stages of error recovery? (RQ2) At which stage transitions does downstream regression most frequently occur after a correct recovery state has been established? By focusing on the preservation and loss of correctness rather than only final success, this study aims to provide a finer-grained account of recovery dynamics and to inform the design of agent systems that can sustain correct reasoning and action throughout the recovery process.
